The Memory Illusion - How Recall Changes Memory

P
Putera Buana Gani
August 24, 2026 8 min read
Page 2 of 3
Photo: Unsplash / Neuroscience research

Remembering does more than reveal a memory. It can make that memory temporarily flexible. When recalled experience is active again, it may be strengthened, connected to new information, or stored in an altered form. Researchers describe this broad process as reconsolidation.

The implication is subtle: the memories we revisit most often are not necessarily the most pristine. They may be the most rehearsed versions, shaped by every retelling, reaction, photograph, and later piece of knowledge that joined the original trace.

Suggestion

A leading question can introduce details that later feel native to the remembered event.

Repetition

A story becomes fluent through retelling, and fluency can be mistaken for accuracy.

Emotion

Current feelings change which details are accessible and how their meaning is interpreted.

Identity

People favor versions of the past that fit the person they believe themselves to be now.

The Misinformation Effect

Experimental psychology has repeatedly shown that information encountered after an event can influence later reports of that event. A question that presupposes a detail may change what a participant later believes was present. The effect matters wherever memory is treated as evidence, especially eyewitness interviews, classrooms, therapy, and conflict between people.

The lesson is not that every recollection is false. It is that the way we ask matters. Open prompts generally protect recall better than questions that contain an expected answer. Hearing other witnesses before giving an independent account can also blend social information with personal memory.

Photographs Help and Interfere

Photographs appear to anchor memory, and often they do preserve details that would otherwise disappear. Yet they also narrow the remembered event around what the camera captured. Over time, a picture can become the dominant visual representation, displacing perspectives, sounds, and moments that occurred outside its frame.

Digital archives amplify this effect. Platforms resurface selected anniversaries, faces, and highlights, repeatedly rehearsing some parts of life while leaving others quiet. The resulting autobiography may feel complete even though it reflects what was photographed, uploaded, and algorithmically returned.

Forgetting Is Part of Intelligence

Forgetting is frustrating, but a system that retained everything equally would struggle to generalize. Details lose strength while patterns remain. Similar experiences blend into knowledge about how the world usually works. This compression supports judgment, language, and prediction.

Memory succeeds not by preserving the past perfectly, but by making the past useful. Its errors often emerge from the same operations that create meaning: selection, association, updating, and abstraction.
