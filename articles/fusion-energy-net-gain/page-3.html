Fusion Energy: The Long Road

P
Putera Buana Gani
June 30, 2026 10 min read
Page 3 of 3
Photo: Unsplash / Fusion physics

So how far away is fusion, really? The honest answer is that the science is no longer the main obstacle — engineering and economics are. And those are harder to schedule than a physics experiment, because they must work not once, in a lab, but continuously, cheaply, for decades.

From Physics to Plant

A working fusion power station has to solve problems the demonstrations sidestep entirely: materials that survive years of neutron bombardment without becoming brittle, a way to breed the fuel isotope tritium on site, and a cost per kilowatt-hour that competes with solar panels which keep getting cheaper every year. None of these is impossible. All of them are unglamorous, expensive, and slow.

"Fusion has stopped being a question of whether the physics works. It is now a question of whether the engineering can be made affordable in time to matter."

That last phrase — in time to matter — is the real stakes. Fusion is not a solution to the current climate crisis; even optimists do not expect meaningful grid power before the 2040s. It is instead a candidate for the century-scale energy foundation that comes after we have already decarbonised with the tools we have now. Both timelines are true at once, and confusing them is the source of most bad fusion arguments.

If you are a citizen: support the patient public funding that de-risks the science, and be sceptical of any startup promising power to your home this decade. If you are a scientist or engineer: the frontier here is materials, tritium breeding, and reliability — the deep, difficult work that turns a spark into a source. The star, at last, is within reach. Holding it steady is the work of a generation.
