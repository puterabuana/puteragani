Designing for Mistakes - Recovery Before Blame

P
Putera Buana Gani
October 3, 2026 7 min read
Page 2 of 3
Photo: Unsplash / Visual design choices

When an interface says Invalid input, it describes the system's judgment but leaves the person's task unfinished. The user still has to discover which field failed, what a valid answer looks like, and whether any work was saved.

A useful error message carries three pieces of information: what happened, what remains intact, and what to do next. It uses language close to the user's goal. For example: We could not save your address. Your other details are still here. Check the postal code and try again.

Match the safeguard to the cost

Low costKeep the action quick and provide a simple undo when possible.
Medium costShow a clear summary before commitment and keep an editable draft.
High costRequire deliberate confirmation and explain irreversible consequences in plain words.

Prevent Errors at the Point of Decision

Prevention is more effective when it appears where a decision is made. A destructive action should look different from a routine one. A date field should show the expected format before a mistake occurs. A payment screen should summarize the amount and destination before the final step.

Confirmation dialogs are useful only when they carry real information. If every minor action produces the same generic question, people learn to dismiss the dialog without reading it. For consequential actions, state the object, the outcome, and whether the action can be reversed.

Undo Is Often Better Than Interruption

For reversible actions, a brief undo window can protect users without blocking their flow. Moving a message to an archive, removing a tag, or changing a preference can often happen immediately with a visible way back.

Undo is not suitable for every task. A sent payment or a public disclosure may have consequences outside the product. Here, the interface needs stronger checks before commitment. The design choice depends on the actual cost of the mistake, not on a blanket preference for speed.

Design for More Than One Way to Notice

Color alone cannot explain a failure. Pair it with text near the relevant control, a clear summary when several fields need attention, and focus behavior that helps keyboard and screen reader users find the problem. The message should remain visible long enough to act on it.

Accessibility improves recovery for everyone. A person using a phone in bright light, a hurried customer, and someone navigating with assistive technology all benefit when the next step is stated plainly and placed where it is needed.
